\begin{abstract}
Hospitals have huge amounts of data about patients, yet sharing data for training machine learning models poses an impractical solution. Why is that the case? Both the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) prohibit hospitals from aggregating patient information on a single server. Conversely, Federated Learning (FL) allows the hospitals to train the machine learning model utilizing their own data while sharing the model’s weights with the coordinating server only. Despite the benefits of the FL methodology, very few studies have evaluated this approach on realistic healthcare datasets.

This paper proposes a research methodology that evaluates FL on real-life clinical datasets. Specifically, we employ the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset (768 samples, 8 clinical features, 34.9\% positive label) using a non-uniform partition based on Dirichlet Non-IID data distribution (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)) simulated on a set of hospital nodes. We employ differential privacy in gradient exchange via Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) and Byzantine-resilient schemes to counter adversarial attacks (Krum, Median).

The most important decision we made in designing our system was putting safety before accuracy. By tuning our thresholds and feature interactions correctly, our federated learning system managed to get a Recall score of 73.70\% (\( \pm \)16.95\%) in a difficult scenario like the Non-IID setting, which is not far off from the 87.04\% achieved by a centralized system. In addition, we mapped the full spectrum of privacy/utility trade-offs, finding that differential privacy SGD does indeed impose a performance penalty, albeit robustness techniques offer some protection against adversaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Information gathering from clinics has never been as abundant before, including everything from medical records, images, and laboratory reports, but nothing goes beyond the walls of these institutions \cite{nguyen2022federated}. The reasons are quite apparent. Regulatory measures, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the USA and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU, clearly state that no information can be uploaded into an external server \cite{price2019privacy, wiens2019do}. At the same time, attacks using ransomware have been targeting the IT systems of hospitals, increasingly putting even authorized cloud storage at high risk \cite{mothukuri2021survey}. It all means that every individual clinic possesses its own data silos. Machine learning models rely on large sets of information to analyze and extrapolate data, but they cannot access anything.

Federated Learning (FL) was precisely invented to address this problem \cite{mcmahan2017communication}. The idea is straightforward: data stays within the network. Hospitals individually construct their versions of the model and provide weight updates obtained through local training \cite{kairouz2021advances}. Given that patient data never leaves the hospital, it is clear that the approach is HIPAA and GDPR compliant by design. Some iterations of this idea have been successfully experimented on datasets of electronic health records \cite{xu2021federated}, physiological measurements from wearable devices \cite{nguyen2022federated}, and multicenter clinical trials \cite{antunes2022federated, sheller2020federated}.

However, when moving from the lab setting to the real-world networks of hospitals, a fundamental issue arises. A large number of FL methods, including Federated Averaging (FedAvg), were invented based on the premise of a perfectly organized and cleaned dataset with Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) property \cite{li2020federated_challenges}. This is far from the reality of patients in the real world. A diabetes clinic in an urban area and a general practice hospital in a rural one attract entirely different patients; thus, their local datasets would be Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) \cite{zhao2018federated}. Simply averaging weights sent by hospitals that are essentially pulling the model apart results in instability \cite{fedprox, scaffold}. In medical applications, the imbalance between disease classes is particularly pronounced \cite{li2020federated}.

Also, the security aspect should be considered. FL doesn't transmit raw data, but the gradient information, which passes through the network, can hardly be defined as metadata. As shown by Zhu et al., under the right conditions, it becomes possible to mathematically reverse engineer the gradients to re-create the training records \cite{zhu2019deep}. This security threat will continue to exist as long as the system doesn't use any mathematically founded privacy constraints \cite{dwork2014algorithmic, abadi2016deep}.

Based on these interrelated problems, we have proposed a privacy-focused FL framework that has been tested on the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset \cite{smith1988using}. Our contribution includes as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Construction of realistic hospital datasets by employing the Non-IID scheme through use of the Dirichlet distribution with the parameter \( \alpha \) set at 0.5 for achieving the same demographic disparity seen in clinical network situations.
    
    \item Application of the Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) technique together with Byzantine-resistant aggregation (Krum, Median) to defend against data poisoning.

    \item Prioritization of diagnostic Recall (achieving 87.04\% in our centralized baseline test) over gross accuracy to improve patient safety, with the full privacy-utility curve plotted for budgets \( \epsilon \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \).
\end{itemize}

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II gives an overview of the related work regarding federated healthcare models and their limitations. Our methodology, including data partitioning and privacy defenses, is detailed in Section~III. Scalability tests alongside the evaluation of our results are covered in Section~IV. Finally, the conclusions and future directions can be found in Section~V.
\section{Related Work}

The utility of FL in the health sector is clear because the hospital can collaborate in developing the models without sharing any patient information. Some organizations have successfully implemented this idea. For instance, Rieke et al.\ \cite{rieke2020future} outlined the development of FL in digital medicine; Sheller et al.\ \cite{sheller2020brain} successfully employed federated brain tumor segmentation where no imaging data was exchanged between organizations; while Biswas et al.\ \cite{biswas2025flpnexainet} recently used FL together with explainable AI for pneumonia screening from GAN-enhanced chest X-ray. Similar optimistic trends are observable in systematic reviews by Nguyen et al.\ \cite{nguyen2022federated} and Antunes et al.\ \cite{antunes2022federated}, which observe clear advancements in the use of multi-site data. However, both systematic reviews also highlight the disconcerting fact that existing optimization algorithms do not perform well when applied to clinical data. This was the case in the study by Xu et al.\ \cite{xu2021federated}, who implemented the federated EHR pipeline successfully only under controlled settings, with the predictive capacity dropping as soon as there were noticeable demographic differences between the participating hospitals.

What factors contribute to the complexity of optimizing federated healthcare data? One of these is the presence of class imbalance: there are many cases where healthy people prevail in certain hospitals, while other hospitals focus on the very problem that needs to be solved by the learning model. In this situation, we obtain a Non-IID dataset. It has been shown that, in this case, convergence guarantees for FedAvg no longer hold true \cite{fedavg_convergence}. It is important to note that this problem has been numerically studied by Zhao et al.\ \cite{zhao2018federated}; the accuracy dropped by up to 55\% as compared to the baseline approach in a Non-IID setting. The proposed solutions include FedProx with regularizer \cite{fedprox} or SCAFFOLD with control variate-based corrections \cite{scaffold}. However, as one can see from all such benchmarks, averaging without any correction simply does not work here \cite{li2020federated, li2020federated_challenges}.

Statistical Heterogeneity is not the whole picture, however. There are also deliberate attacks against federated networks. Mothukuri et al.\ \cite{mothukuri2021survey} categorize FL attacks in two ways: Data poisoning, which occurs when an adversarial client pollutes their own dataset, and model poisoning, where the updates being transmitted are affected. But there is another, more subtle threat which, to me, is even more alarming. Zhu et al.\ \cite{zhu2019deep} found that if one is given enough snapshots of the gradients, it is possible to reconstruct training data via mathematical inversion---without ever having seen the data itself. In order to prevent such attacks, Byzantine-robust aggregation techniques are now employed. Bulyan \cite{mhamdi2018bulyan}, a heavy-weight method, provides excellent theoretical guarantees, but it is very costly computation-wise, and too costly for the relatively under-powered clinical server rooms in which these systems operate. Light-weight methods have been more successful. Krum \cite{blanchard2017machine} selects the update nearest to the other clients' updates, while Coordinate-wise Median \cite{yin2018byzantine} screens parameters in each direction separately. These methods are computationally light enough to be used alongside Differential Privacy restrictions \cite{abadi2016deep}.

Here’s where the gap lies. Non-IID, Byzantine robustness, and privacy-preserving learning approaches have been individually analyzed and appear to be promising alone \cite{nguyen2022federated}. What happens, however, when you combine all three? What happens to the clinically relevant metrics, particularly Recall? Traditional accuracy measures and ROC analysis may obscure significant blind spots within skewed data; as evidenced by Saito and Rehmsmeier, precision-recall analysis paints a much clearer picture \cite{saito2015precision}. This paper bridges the gap. We implement a pipeline using Non-IID partitioning, DP-SGD, and Byzantine aggregation while evaluating our approach in relation to the clinically important metric of Recall.
\section{Methodology}

Our design focused on stressing the FL setting more similar to what is encountered in a typical hospital environment than in a benchmark. Our approach involves four key decisions: how the distributed simulation was constructed, the nature of the splits that generate realistic demographic distribution among patients, which server-side privacy techniques we apply, and finally, how we formalize these settings with additional formal privacy guarantees.

\subsection{System Architecture and Reproducibility}

The simulation was implemented with Python and Scikit-Learn. Each simulated hospital trains a Logistic Regression with L-BFGS solver, a learning rate \( \eta = 0.01 \) and up to 2000 iterations. When the local training process finishes, the hospital transfers its trained parameters to the server, not any patient data. By default, there are five hospital clients and a single server involved in federated rounds; the simulation runs for 10 rounds, and uses CPU-only computation, as is typically the case with the hardware at hand.

Reproducibility is critical for experimental results. Each test involves random seeding with a fixed value of 42 and a stratified 80/20 train/test split. To ensure that the results obtained do not reflect some peculiarities of the data split, each test was run for five independent Non-IID partitions (seeds \{42, 123, 456, 789, 2024\}) with average \( \pm \) standard deviation reported.

\subsection{Distributed Patient Cohorts (Non-IID Modeling)}

Typical machine learning algorithms assume that training samples are identically and independently distributed. Hospitals violate this assumption entirely. A department of endocrinology at a university and a general practice in the countryside represent two completely different distributions, each having vastly differing rates of diabetic patients. To simulate this effect, we used the well-known Pima Indians Diabetes Database---a total of 768 patients, 8 patient attributes, 34.9\% diabetic. In our scenario, this dataset was artificially divided among clients according to the Dirichlet distribution with a very low alpha value (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)). This deliberately lopsided division means that one hypothetical hospital may contain mostly diabetic patients while another will have hardly any.

\subsection{Aggregation Techniques for Federated Learning}

The easiest way to combine the updates made by clients is to use the averaging technique -- specifically, federated averaging (FedAvg). It is effective; however, there are two problems associated with it. First, it is prone to being affected by a lack of homogeneity in the data distribution between clients, as it enables the vast majority of the population to dominate the global weights. Second, a single adversarial client can poison the global weights by introducing an erroneous update.

We tested two types of aggregations that, in theory, can prevent such an attack. The first type involves taking a coordinate-wise median of the provided weight vectors instead of averaging, which will effectively eliminate any outliers. The second method, called Krum, involves calculating the Euclidean distance between all of the submitted vectors and choosing the one that has the smallest distance to its closest neighbors. In other words, this technique ensures that only the values that represent consensus are chosen and outliers are discarded. In our experiment, we consciously chose not to implement FedProx or SCAFFOLD to see how geometric aggregation defenses would interact with DP noise.

\subsection{Privacy Issues with Differential Privacy}

Privacy protection techniques were applied formally on the client side via the employment of the DP-SGD algorithm, which applies the differential privacy mechanism proposed by Abadi et al.\ \cite{abadi2016deep} and has recently shown promise in healthcare contexts \cite{hasan2025privacy}. More specifically, before the updated weights are sent to the server, the addition of Gaussian noise with the appropriate parameters in the gradients of each hospital should take place. In order to limit the impact of a single record on the final output, the clipping norm of 1.0 per sample was selected. Hence, under the default settings of \( \epsilon = 1.0 \) and \( \delta = 0.01 \), the attacker would not be able to learn anything useful regarding a specific individual, this being the formal privacy guarantee offered by the DP-SGD algorithm.

The following five values of \( \epsilon \) were explored: \( \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \). The cost for such privacy turned out to be rather high. Namely, under the settings of \( \epsilon = 1.0 \), Accuracy decreased from the non-private federated baseline of 70.91\% to 62.34\% (\( \pm \)6.43\%), while Recall dropped to 52.22\% (\( \pm \)15.86\%) down from 73.70\%. This can be attributed to the relatively large amount of Gaussian noise (i.e. \( \sigma \approx 3.03 \)) introduced into the model that has a small parameter space (i.e. 19 features).

In the realm of clinical practice, however, a misdiagnosis is an extremely critical issue. False positives, meaning that healthy patients are flagged as possible diabetics, only lead to further testing; false negatives, which mean a sick patient leaves without treatment, are more problematic. This was the premise of every decision we made; we focused on Recall instead of accuracy.

\subsection{Baseline Clinical Model vs. Federated Learning}

We used two equivalent data preprocessing methods to level the playing field between the centralized and federated pipelines. Both included the same set of 11 feature engineering procedures (5 interaction terms, 4 polynomial terms, and 2 ratio features such as Glucose \( \times \) BMI) and the same threshold value of 0.30. We got 69.48\% accuracy and 87.04\% Recall, or 7 mistakes out of 54 total diabetic patients. This is our theoretical limit.

What does federating do? FedAvg resulted in 70.91\% (\( \pm \)1.90\%) accuracy without DP for 5 Non-IID Dirichlet partitioned clients (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)), which was surprisingly better than centralizing, but Recall was highly noisy, resulting in 73.70\% (\( \pm \)16.95\%) on average with an average of 14.2 (\( \pm \)9.2) patients missed on diagnosis. Certain Dirichlet partitions are better than others; when the minority class patients are concentrated on only one or two clients, it is hard for the global model to learn the positive class boundaries. We saw that in all the partitions, convergence became saturated by round 10. In other words (Table~\ref{tab:performance}), while Federated models were never better than Centralized Recall models, they came close enough to be useful---and patients' information never left their institutions.

\subsection{Privacy and Scalability Trade-Offs}

Incorporating DP-SGD on Non-IID Partitioning naturally comes at a cost of its own. Naturally. As is clear from Table~\ref{tab:performance}, bringing \( \epsilon \) down from 10.0 to 1.0 results in a Recall decrease from 78.89\% to 52.22\%. While the model only uses 19 parameters, the Gaussian noise added by DP outweighs the actual signal. Researchers working on applying DP in small tabular healthcare applications need to take note of this weakness.

For scalability, we performed experiments varying the number of clients from 5 to 20 (Fig.~\ref{fig:comprehensive}). The communication overhead increased linearly---a behavior anticipated (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). However, Recall had something surprising to reveal. All settings but 15 clients failed to preserve Recall above the 80\% recall rate threshold. The fact of the matter is that for a small dataset size of 768 records, certain numbers of clients will lead to partitioning through Dirichlet, leaving the local datasets insufficient for proper learning. Specifically, there is a visible drop in Recall when scaling from 15 to 20 clients; at 20 clients, the dataset becomes too fragmented, and each hospital receives too few samples (especially of the minority positive class) to train a reliable local model.

We additionally tried performing dropout simulations with unexpected client failures in the process of aggregation. In every attempt, the server managed to cope with the problem and succeed in aggregating the remaining clients (Fig.~\ref{fig:bottleneck}). Just an observation of a practical nature: Byzantine-resilient approaches, including Krum, needed 15--20\% more rounds of communication than FedAvg alone to achieve convergence of the model.

\section{Conclusion}

What did we learn? Firstly, we showed that FL can achieve equal accuracy compared to the centralized learning method (70.91\% versus 69.48\%) without disclosing a single patient’s record from its hospital -- an absolute necessity in the context of HIPAA and GDPR. Secondly, and even more importantly, accuracy by itself should not be considered an appropriate measure for evaluating the performance of the clinical ML. By tuning the model threshold and designing appropriate feature interactions, both our centrally trained and federated models reached clinically acceptable Recall levels; otherwise, their accuracy scores would not convey the real information about their usefulness.

Less pleasant conclusion to draw is about the cost of privacy. When the value of \( \epsilon \) equals 1.0, DP-SGD degrades the Recall from 73.70\% to 52.22\%. In the case of 19 features in a dataset, the introduced noise level exceeds the actual signal. Table~\ref{tab:performance} contains the full tradeoff between the two, so that researchers could choose the level of privacy that meets their institution’s requirements.

What lies ahead? The natural direction for future work is the experiment with more complex clinical datasets -- multiple modalities, imaging, and genomic features -- where the ratio of noise to data becomes smaller.
