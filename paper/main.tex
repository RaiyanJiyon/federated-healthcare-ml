\begin{abstract}
Hospitals collect enormous amounts of patient data, yet sharing it for training diagnostic models remains impractical. Why? Privacy laws---the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)---forbid pooling clinical records on any central server. Federated Learning (FL) bypasses this barrier: each hospital trains a model on its own data and sends only learned parameters, not patient records, to a coordinating server. Despite this promise, few studies have pushed FL against the hard, unglamorous realities of clinical datasets.

In this paper, we build and test such a system. We use the Pima Indians Diabetes Database (768 samples, 8 clinical features, 34.9\% positive class) and split it across simulated hospital nodes with a Dirichlet-based Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) partition (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)) to mimic real demographic gaps. We protect gradient transmissions with Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) and defend against adversarial poisoning through Byzantine-robust aggregation (Krum, Median).

Our most important design decision was to optimize for clinical safety rather than headline accuracy. With threshold calibration and engineered feature interactions, the federated model reached a diagnostic Recall of 73.70\% (\( \pm \)16.95\%) under Non-IID conditions---not far from the 87.04\% centralized baseline. We also map out the full privacy--utility trade-off: DP-SGD does exact a real performance cost on this small dataset, but robust aggregation provides tangible resistance to Byzantine attacks. The paper offers a reproducible, warts-and-all benchmark for federated healthcare pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Clinical facilities today generate patient data at an unprecedented rate---electronic records, imaging archives, laboratory panels---but almost none of it flows between institutions \cite{nguyen2022federated}. The reasons are well known. Data protection frameworks like the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union explicitly forbid shipping raw patient records to any external server \cite{price2019privacy, wiens2019do}. At the same time, ransomware campaigns targeting hospital IT infrastructure have multiplied, making even authorized centralized storage increasingly risky \cite{mothukuri2021survey}. The net effect is that every clinic holds its own data island. Machine learning models that need broad, multi-site training sets to generalize simply cannot access them.

Federated Learning (FL) was proposed precisely to work around this constraint \cite{mcmahan2017communication}. The idea is straightforward: data never moves. Instead, every participating hospital trains a local copy of the model and transmits only the resulting weight updates to a central server, which fuses them into a shared global model \cite{kairouz2021advances}. No individual patient record ever leaves its home institution, so the scheme is compatible with HIPAA and GDPR by construction. Researchers have already tested variants of this approach on electronic health records \cite{xu2021federated}, wearable physiological monitoring \cite{nguyen2022federated}, and multi-hospital clinical trials \cite{antunes2022federated, sheller2020federated}.

Moving from controlled experiments to actual hospital networks, however, uncovers a stubborn problem. Most FL algorithms---Federated Averaging (FedAvg) chief among them---were designed with well-curated, Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) benchmarks in mind \cite{li2020federated_challenges}. Real clinical populations look nothing like that. A diabetes clinic in a metropolitan area and a rural general practice serve wildly different patient demographics; their local datasets are, statistically speaking, deeply Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) \cite{zhao2018federated}. When a server naively averages weights from such mismatched clients, the optimization process can destabilize: different hospitals effectively tug the model in opposite directions \cite{fedprox, scaffold}. In healthcare, where disease class ratios vary enormously from site to site, this problem is especially acute \cite{li2020federated}.

There is also a security angle that deserves attention. FL does avoid moving raw data, but the gradient updates that \emph{do} travel across the network are not harmless metadata. Zhu et al.\ demonstrated that, under favorable conditions, an adversary can mathematically invert shared gradients to reconstruct the original training records \cite{zhu2019deep}. Unless the system enforces mathematically grounded privacy limits, this vulnerability persists \cite{dwork2014algorithmic, abadi2016deep}.

Motivated by these overlapping challenges, we built and evaluated a privacy-hardened FL pipeline on the Pima Indians Diabetes Database \cite{smith1988using}. Our three main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We simulate realistic hospital data silos through a Dirichlet-based Non-IID partitioning scheme with \( \alpha = 0.5 \), producing the kind of demographic imbalance that real clinical networks exhibit.
    \item We deploy Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) together with Byzantine-resilient aggregation (Krum, Median), giving the system layered protection against both gradient reconstruction attacks and malicious data poisoning.
    \item We deliberately optimize for diagnostic Recall rather than headline accuracy, achieving 87.04\% Recall in the centralized baseline and mapping the full privacy--utility curve across budgets \( \epsilon \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \).
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews the relevant literature. Section~III describes our experimental setup---data partitioning, aggregation rules, and privacy mechanisms. Section~IV presents the results, including scalability experiments. Section~V concludes the paper and suggests future directions.

\section{Related Work}

The appeal of FL for healthcare is obvious: hospitals can collaborate on model training without ever handing over their patient data. Several groups have shown this works in practice. Rieke et al.\ \cite{rieke2020future} laid out a roadmap for FL in digital medicine; Sheller et al.\ \cite{sheller2020brain} demonstrated federated brain tumour segmentation where no imaging data crossed institutional lines; and more recently, Biswas et al.\ \cite{biswas2025flpnexainet} paired FL with explainable AI to screen for pneumonia on GAN-augmented chest X-rays. Broad surveys by Nguyen et al.\ \cite{nguyen2022federated} and Antunes et al.\ \cite{antunes2022federated} paint a similarly encouraging picture, noting clear gains in multi-site data utilization. But both surveys also flag an uncomfortable truth: off-the-shelf optimization methods tend to break down once clinical data enters the picture. Xu et al.\ \cite{xu2021federated} saw this firsthand---their federated EHR pipeline worked well under controlled conditions but lost predictive power as soon as the participating hospitals differed significantly in patient demographics.

Why does healthcare data cause so much trouble for federated optimization? Two words: class imbalance. One hospital might see mostly healthy patients; another might specialize in the very condition the model is trying to detect. The resulting local datasets are emphatically Non-IID. Under these conditions, the convergence guarantees of FedAvg no longer hold \cite{fedavg_convergence}. Zhao et al.\ \cite{zhao2018federated} measured the damage in a controlled experiment: accuracy dropped by as much as 55\% compared to a centralized IID baseline. The field has proposed fixes---FedProx adds a regularization penalty to prevent local models from wandering too far from the global state \cite{fedprox}, while SCAFFOLD uses control variates to compensate for per-client drift \cite{scaffold}---but benchmarking on heavily skewed partitions consistently shows that uncorrected averaging falls apart in the fragmented data world of hospital networks \cite{li2020federated, li2020federated_challenges}.

Statistical heterogeneity is only half the story. Federated networks also face deliberate attacks. Mothukuri et al.\ \cite{mothukuri2021survey} sort FL threats into two buckets: data poisoning (a rogue client contaminates its own training set) and model poisoning (the transmitted weight updates themselves are tampered with). A subtler risk, arguably scarier, was uncovered by Zhu et al.\ \cite{zhu2019deep}: given enough gradient snapshots, an attacker can sometimes reconstruct individual training records through mathematical inversion---even when no raw data was ever shared. To limit the damage, researchers have turned to Byzantine-robust aggregation. Heavy-duty approaches like Bulyan \cite{mhamdi2018bulyan} provide strong guarantees in theory, but they are computationally expensive---too expensive for the modest hardware that most hospital server rooms run on. Lighter methods have proven more practical. Krum \cite{blanchard2017machine} picks the single client update that sits closest to the crowd, while coordinate-wise Median \cite{yin2018byzantine} filters out outliers one parameter dimension at a time. Both are cheap enough to run alongside Differential Privacy constraints \cite{abadi2016deep} on resource-limited clinical edge nodes.

Here is the gap. Non-IID handling, Byzantine defenses, and formal privacy have each been studied on their own, and each looks promising in isolation \cite{nguyen2022federated}. But what happens when you stack all three on top of one another? And what happens to the metrics that actually matter in medicine---specifically, Recall? Standard accuracy and ROC curves can hide dangerous blind spots in imbalanced clinical datasets; as Saito and Rehmsmeier showed, precision--recall evaluation is far more revealing \cite{saito2015precision}. Our study fills this gap. We combine Non-IID partitioning, DP-SGD, and Byzantine-robust aggregation in a single pipeline and anchor every evaluation to diagnostic Recall---the number that tells a clinician how many sick patients the model would have missed.

\section{Methodology}
We designed our experiments to stress-test FL under conditions that look more like a real hospital network than a textbook benchmark. Four decisions shaped the setup: how we organized the distributed simulation, how we split the data to create realistic demographic variation, which server-side aggregation defenses we used, and how we added formal privacy guarantees on top of everything.

\subsection{System Architecture and Reproducibility}
We built the simulation in Python using Scikit-Learn. Each virtual hospital runs a Logistic Regression model with the L-BFGS solver, a learning rate of \( \eta = 0.01 \), and up to 2000 iterations. After training finishes locally, the hospital sends its updated model coefficients---not any patient data---to a central server that merges them. In the default setup, five hospital clients talk to one server across 10 federated rounds; we ran everything on CPU-only machines, since that is the kind of hardware most hospitals actually have.

Reproducibility matters. Every experiment uses a fixed random seed of 42 and an $80/20$ stratified train--test split. To make sure our results are not just an artifact of one lucky data partition, we repeat every measurement across 5 independent Non-IID splits (seeds \{42, 123, 456, 789, 2024\}) and report the mean \( \pm \) standard deviation.

\subsection{Distributed Patient Cohorts (Non-IID Modeling)}
Standard ML assumes all training examples come from the same distribution. Hospitals violate that assumption completely. A university endocrinology department and a rural general practice serve very different populations, with very different diabetes rates. To capture this, we took the Pima Indians Diabetes Database---768 patients, 8 clinical features, 34.9\% diabetic---and split it across clients using a Dirichlet distribution (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)). The low \( \alpha \) value produces lopsided splits on purpose: one virtual hospital might end up with mostly diabetic patients while another gets almost none. This is not a bug; it is the point. The resulting local class imbalance tests whether the global model can still learn useful patterns when individual hospitals give it a very skewed view of the world.

\subsection{Federated Aggregation Strategies}
The simplest way for the server to merge client updates is plain averaging---Federated Averaging, or \textit{FedAvg}. It works, but it has two weaknesses. First, when clients have very different data distributions, averaging can let the majority clients dominate the global model. Second, a single malicious participant can corrupt the whole aggregate.

We therefore tested two alternatives that are designed to resist such corruption. \textit{Median} aggregation replaces the mean with the coordinate-wise median of the incoming weight vectors; outliers are automatically suppressed. \textit{Krum} works differently: it computes the pairwise Euclidean distances among all client submissions and picks the one with the smallest total distance to its neighbors---in effect, it trusts the update that best represents the group consensus and throws out anything suspicious. We deliberately did not include heavier-weight methods like FedProx or SCAFFOLD in this round of experiments. The reason is isolation: we wanted to measure how geometric aggregation defenses interact with Differential Privacy noise without confounding the picture with additional local optimization corrections.

\subsection{Differential Privacy Constraints}
We added formal privacy protection at the client level using DP-SGD, following the framework introduced by Abadi et al.\ \cite{abadi2016deep} and adapted for medical imaging by Hasan and Rahman \cite{hasan2025privacy}. In practical terms, before any hospital sends its updated weights to the server, calibrated Gaussian noise is injected into the gradient vector. A per-sample clipping norm of 1.0 bounds how much any single patient record can influence the update. With our default settings (\( \epsilon = 1.0 \), \( \delta = 0.01 \)), an attacker who intercepts the transmitted updates learns almost nothing about individual patients---that is the formal guarantee.

We tested five privacy budgets: \( \epsilon \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \). The trade-off is real and significant. At \( \epsilon = 1.0 \), accuracy fell from 70.91\% (the non-private federated baseline) to 62.34\% (\( \pm \)6.43\%), and Recall dropped from 73.70\% to 52.22\% (\( \pm \)15.86\%). The reason is straightforward: the noise magnitude (\( \sigma \approx 3.03 \)) is large relative to the model's small parameter space (19 features). When you have very few parameters, even moderate noise can swamp the actual signal. Table~\ref{tab:performance} shows the full privacy--utility curve.

In clinical practice, a missed diagnosis can be catastrophic. A false alarm---flagging a healthy patient as possibly diabetic---triggers additional tests, which is inconvenient but not dangerous. A false negative, on the other hand, means a genuinely sick patient walks away untreated. That asymmetry guided every evaluation decision we made: we optimized for Recall, not accuracy.

\subsection{Clinical Baseline vs. Federated Optimization}
To keep the comparison fair, we ran both the centralized and federated pipelines through identical preprocessing: the same 11 engineered features (5 interaction terms, 4 polynomial features, 2 ratio features---for example, Glucose \( \times \) BMI), and the same clinical decision threshold of 0.30. The centralized model, trained on all 768 patients at once, reached 69.48\% accuracy and 87.04\% Recall, missing 7 out of 54 diabetic patients. That is our ceiling---the best we can do without privacy constraints.

What happens when we federate? With 5 Non-IID Dirichlet-partitioned clients (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)) and no DP, FedAvg produced 70.91\% (\( \pm \)1.90\%) accuracy---slightly higher than centralized, interestingly---but Recall was much noisier: 73.70\% (\( \pm \)16.95\%), meaning on average 14.2 (\( \pm \)9.2) missed diagnoses across our 5 partition trials. Some Dirichlet splits are kinder than others; when minority-class patients cluster in just one or two clients, the global model struggles to learn positive-class boundaries. We confirmed that convergence plateaued by round 10 in every trial. The bottom line (see Table~\ref{tab:performance}): the federated model does not beat centralized Recall, but it comes close enough to be operationally useful---and no patient data ever leaves its home institution.

\subsection{Privacy and Scalability Trade-Offs}
Adding DP-SGD on top of Non-IID partitioning costs performance. There is no way around it. Table~\ref{tab:performance} tells the story clearly: going from \( \epsilon = 10.0 \) down to \( \epsilon = 1.0 \) pushes mean Recall from 78.89\% to 52.22\%. With only 19 model parameters, the Gaussian noise that DP injects is large relative to the signal the model is trying to learn. Practitioners considering DP for small tabular healthcare datasets should be aware of this limitation.

We also tested scalability by increasing the number of participating clients from 5 to 20 (Fig.~\ref{fig:comprehensive}). Communication cost scaled linearly---no surprises there (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). Recall, however, told a less predictable story. Only the 15-client configuration consistently held above the 80\% clinical safety line; configurations with 5, 7, 10, and 20 clients all dipped below. The honest answer is that with just 768 total records, Dirichlet partitioning at certain client counts creates local datasets that are simply too small or too skewed for the aggregated model to recover from. We also ran dropout simulations---pulling clients out mid-round without warning---and the server handled it gracefully every time, completing aggregation with the remaining participants (Fig.~\ref{fig:bottleneck}). One practical note: Byzantine-robust methods, especially Krum, needed roughly 15--20\% more communication rounds than plain FedAvg to reach the same convergence point. That is the price of safety.

\section{Conclusion}
What did we learn? First, FL can match centralized accuracy on a diabetes prediction task (70.91\% vs.\ 69.48\%) without any patient record ever leaving its hospital---a hard requirement under HIPAA and GDPR. Second, and more importantly, accuracy by itself is a poor metric for clinical ML. Threshold tuning and engineered feature interactions got both the centralized and federated models to clinically useful Recall levels; without those steps, raw accuracy numbers would have been misleading.

The less comfortable finding is the cost of privacy. At \( \epsilon = 1.0 \), DP-SGD dropped Recall from 73.70\% to 52.22\%. On a dataset with only 19 features, the noise simply overwhelms the signal. Table~\ref{tab:performance} lays out the full trade-off curve so that practitioners can pick the privacy budget that matches their institution's risk tolerance.

Where do we go from here? The obvious next step is to run these same experiments on larger, richer clinical datasets---multi-modal records, imaging, genomics. With more features, the relative impact of DP noise shrinks. That could be the path to strong privacy guarantees that do not sacrifice the diagnostic safety patients depend on.
