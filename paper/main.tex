\begin{abstract}
Privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR restrict multi-center pooling of raw electronic health records, while single-institution datasets are often insufficient for robust clinical prediction. This paper presents an empirical benchmark of federated learning (FL) for Intensive Care Unit (ICU) mortality prediction under Non-IID conditions. We compare server-side aggregation rules (FedAvg, FedProx, coordinate-wise Median, Krum, and a validation-weighted variant, FedF2), per-sample differential privacy (DP-SGD, $\epsilon=4.36$, $\delta=10^{-5}$), and data-poisoning attacks. MIMIC-IV (65,273 ICU admissions, 31 features) is partitioned into seven care-unit clients. Centralized logistic regression reaches 0.8920 AUROC; federated logistic regression reaches 0.8784 (1.52\% relative loss). However, after Platt scaling at the centralized threshold ($\tau=0.39$), recall falls from 85.2\% to 43.5\%, and recovering 86.3\% recall requires $\tau=0.05$ at 23.9\% precision (centralized: 30.2\%). Exploratory federated XGBoost via ensemble voting, which carries no formal privacy guarantee, reaches 0.9155 AUROC. When three of seven clients flip labels, Median retains 0.8594 AUROC and Krum $\approx$0.851 (Median minimum over all 1--3-client attacks: 0.8353), whereas FedAvg falls to 0.5011. DP-SGD yields 0.8477 AUROC. Differential privacy and Byzantine-robust aggregation were evaluated separately, not jointly. A seven-hospital federated replication on eICU-CRD gives 0.8349 AUROC (1.09\% relative loss versus centralized). The results come from single-split simulations; they support the feasibility of FL for ICU mortality prediction but do not establish clinical readiness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The healthcare industry collects medical data in clinical institutions at an unprecedented rate, including electronic health records, radiology images, and laboratory results. However, little of this data is exchanged between healthcare organizations, largely because legislation such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the US and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union restricts sharing patient data outside institutional boundaries~\cite{price2019privacy,wiens2019do}. Ransomware attacks on hospital IT systems further increase the risk of centralized storage~\cite{mothukuri2021survey}. In effect, hospitals hold their own islands of data, and machine learning methods that need large datasets cannot access them~\cite{nguyen2022federated}.

Federated Learning (FL) was proposed to work around this constraint~\cite{mcmahan2017communication}. Every participating hospital trains a local copy of the model and transmits only model updates to a central server, which fuses them into a shared global model~\cite{kairouz2021advances}. No patient record leaves its home institution, which supports compliance with HIPAA and GDPR privacy principles by keeping raw EHR data on local premises. FL variants have been tested on electronic health records~\cite{xu2021federated}, wearable physiological monitoring~\cite{nguyen2022federated}, and multi-hospital studies~\cite{antunes2022federated, sheller2020federated}.

Moving from controlled experiments to hospital networks, however, exposes a problem. Most FL algorithms, Federated Averaging (FedAvg) chief among them, were designed with Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) benchmarks in mind~\cite{li2020federated_challenges}. Clinical populations differ across sites in demographics, case mix, and disease prevalence, so local datasets are typically Non-IID~\cite{zhao2018federated}. When a server averages models from such mismatched clients, optimization can destabilize because different sites pull the model in different directions~\cite{fedprox, scaffold}; in healthcare, where class ratios vary from site to site, this problem is especially acute~\cite{li2020federated}.

There is also a security dimension. FL avoids transmitting raw data, but the updates that are transmitted can leak information: Zhu et al.\ showed that an attacker may, under favorable conditions, reconstruct training records from gradients~\cite{zhu2019deep}. Formal protection requires mathematical guarantees such as differential privacy~\cite{dwork2014algorithmic, abadi2016deep}. Prompted by these concerns, we built a benchmark pipeline for federated ICU mortality prediction using MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV). We create Non-IID client groups by partitioning the data into ICU care units, and we replicate the federated experiment on seven hospitals of the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Benchmark.} We provide an empirical comparison of FedAvg, FedProx, coordinate-wise Median, Krum, and a validation-weighted variant (FedF2) for ICU mortality prediction with seven care-unit clients (a single-institution proxy for Non-IID data), together with a seven-hospital federated replication on eICU-CRD.
\item \textbf{Byzantine robustness.} Under label-flip, sign-flip, and adaptive poisoning of 1--3 of 7 clients, Median (AUROC $\geq 0.8353$ in all scenarios, 0.8594 under three label-flip clients) and Krum ($\approx 0.851$) remain stable, whereas FedAvg, FedProx, and FedF2 collapse under three label-flip clients.
\item \textbf{Operating points and privacy.} We show that federated averaging compresses predicted probabilities, so a threshold transferred from a centralized model loses sensitivity (43.5\% versus 85.2\% recall at $\tau=0.39$); we report a matched-sensitivity comparison, DP-SGD results at one privacy budget, and a negative result for F2-score-based client weighting.
\end{itemize}
We note the scope of these claims: differential privacy and Byzantine-robust aggregation are evaluated in separate experiments, and their interaction is left for future work.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the methodology. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, including the Byzantine, privacy, scalability, and eICU-CRD experiments. Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Federated Learning has emerged as a promising paradigm for collaborative healthcare analytics, enabling multiple institutions to train shared models without exchanging patient records while supporting compliance with privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR. Surveys by Rieke et al.~\cite{rieke2020future}, Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2022federated}, and Antunes et al.~\cite{antunes2022federated} identify FL as a key technology for healthcare AI. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated in brain tumor segmentation~\cite{sheller2020brain}, pneumonia detection with explainable AI~\cite{biswas2025flpnexainet}, COVID-19 outcome prediction across twenty hospitals~\cite{dayan2021federated}, rare cancer segmentation across seventy-one institutions~\cite{pati2022federated}, and EHR analysis~\cite{kaissis2020secure,xu2021federated}. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2025privacy} recently developed a privacy-preserving federated framework for multi-source EHR prognosis prediction using feature calibration. These studies demonstrate feasibility, but they mostly evaluate FL in benign environments and often provide limited analysis of statistical heterogeneity, adversarial robustness, formal privacy guarantees, and external clinical validation. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2021federated}, for instance, reported that federated EHR models degrade noticeably when participating hospitals differ substantially in patient demographics.

Non-IID data is one of the main challenges in healthcare FL. Hospitals differ in patient populations, disease prevalence, and treatment protocols, violating the assumptions behind FedAvg. Li et al.~\cite{li2020convergence} showed theoretically that FedAvg converges more slowly under heterogeneous client distributions, and Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2018federated} observed accuracy degradation of up to 55\% relative to IID training in an extreme label-skew setting. Proposed remedies include FedProx~\cite{fedprox}, SCAFFOLD~\cite{scaffold}, and personalization methods such as Ditto and MAML-based approaches~\cite{li2021ditto,fallah2020personalized}. Many of these add computational complexity or target client personalization rather than a robust global model, and most evaluations use generic benchmark data rather than EHR data. Clinical data add severe class imbalance, missing values, and varying prevalence across hospitals, so the behavior of these methods in ICU settings is insufficiently explored. Our benchmark therefore includes FedProx and builds ICU-specific Non-IID client partitions from MIMIC-IV, followed by a federated replication on eICU-CRD.

Model updates themselves may leak sensitive information. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2019deep} reconstructed training samples from shared gradients, and membership-inference and model-inversion attacks expose information without access to the underlying data~\cite{shokri2016membership,fredrikson2014privacy}. Differential Privacy (DP), in particular DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016deep,dwork2014algorithmic}, is a standard mechanism for formal guarantees, although stronger privacy generally reduces utility and can affect minority classes disproportionately~\cite{bagdasaryan2019differential}. In parallel, Byzantine-resilient aggregation rules such as Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine} and coordinate-wise Median~\cite{yin2018byzantine} defend against malicious client updates at low computational cost. Privacy, robustness, and optimization are usually studied separately, often on synthetic benchmarks. This work contributes an empirical comparison of these components on clinical data. We evaluate DP-SGD and Byzantine-robust aggregation within one pipeline but in separate experiments; their interaction is not evaluated here.

Reliable evaluation is equally important for clinical decision support. ICU mortality data are imbalanced, so accuracy is an inadequate indicator, and precision--recall analysis is more informative than ROC analysis on imbalanced data~\cite{saito2015precision}. Because a missed high-risk patient (false negative) is generally costlier than a false alarm, we report recall and precision at explicit operating points alongside AUROC. Trustworthy deployment also requires external validation, reliable probability estimates, and robustness across heterogeneous hospital populations~\cite{wiens2019do}. Motivated by these considerations, this work benchmarks FL for ICU mortality prediction with Non-IID partitioning of MIMIC-IV, a cross-dataset federated replication on eICU-CRD, DP-SGD, Krum and Median aggregation, and calibration-aware evaluation.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Our experiments subject FL to conditions resembling a hospital network rather than a standard benchmark (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). The design has four parts: the distributed simulation, the data partitioning that creates Non-IID clients, the server-side aggregation rules, and the privacy mechanism.

\subsection{System Architecture and Reproducibility}

The simulation runs in Python on a single workstation, with each client's data held in a separate partition that the server never accesses. Each of the seven clients trains a logistic regression, a multilayer perceptron (MLP), or gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) on its local patient data. DP-SGD, which clips per-sample gradients and adds Gaussian noise to the clipped gradients, is applied only to the logistic-regression pipeline; the MLP and XGBoost experiments are non-private. In each round, clients send model updates to the server, which aggregates them with one of five rules (FedAvg, FedProx, FedF2, Median, or Krum) and returns the global model. Baseline runs use five rounds; the aggregation and robustness runs use twenty rounds.

All primary benchmark results use random seed 42 with stratified train--validation--test splits (yielding the headline FedAvg AUROC of 0.8784 in Table~\ref{tab:performance}). To assess variability across cohort shuffles, we repeated the FedAvg experiment with five seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 1000), obtaining a mean AUROC of $0.8860 \pm 0.0065$. Only FedAvg was repeated; all other results, including Tables~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation} and~\ref{tab:byzantine_robustness}, are single runs with seed 42.

\subsection{Distributed Patient Cohorts (Care-Unit Modeling)}

Patient cohorts differ considerably across care settings: the ICU of an academic center serves a different population than that of a rural community hospital, which violates the IID assumption underlying most learning algorithms. We use MIMIC-IV~\cite{PhysioNet-mimiciv-3.1,johnson2023mimic} (version 3.1), which contains 65,273 ICU admissions characterized by 31 variables: demographics (age, gender, admission type, insurance type), vital signs from the first 24 hours (HR, SBP, MAP, RR, temperature, SpO$_2$, glucose), laboratory measurements (creatinine, BUN, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, hemoglobin, WBC, platelet, lactate, bilirubin, INR), and severity scores (SOFA, SAPS II~\cite{legall1993saps2}, Charlson index~\cite{charlson1987new}). The prediction target is a binary mortality label with a prevalence of about 11\%.
% TODO(authors): state the exact outcome definition (e.g., in-hospital mortality), the unit of analysis (ICU stay vs. patient), imputation, patient-level splitting, and whether the scaler is fit per client or globally.

MIMIC-IV admissions are split into seven ICU care units (MICU, SICU, CCU, etc.), each serving a distinct patient population and thus producing Non-IID local distributions. We use these seven units as the seven federated clients.

\textbf{Scope of simulation.} All seven clients originate from one institution (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center). This allows controlled study of Non-IID effects and adversaries, but it does not reproduce the differences in EHR software, protocols, and demographics found between real hospitals. The eICU-CRD experiments in Section~\ref{subsec:eicu_validation} use geographically separate hospitals, but they train new models on eICU-CRD rather than testing the MIMIC-IV model; they are therefore a replication, not an external validation. End-to-end evaluation on a real multi-hospital deployment is future work~\cite{nishio2019client}.

\subsection{Model Architectures}

To test whether our findings extend beyond linear models, we evaluate three architectures.

\paragraph{Logistic Regression (Baseline).}
We train class-weighted logistic regression (31 features $\rightarrow$ 1 output) with $\ell_2$ regularization to handle the class imbalance (about 11\% positives). The model is explainable, computationally cheap, and a classical choice for clinical prediction.

\paragraph{Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).}
The MLP has architecture $31 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 1$ (4,161 parameters) with ReLU activations, dropout (rate 0.2), and a sigmoid output. It is trained with Adam (learning rate 0.001), binary cross-entropy loss, 20 local epochs per federated round, and batch size 32, using the same splits and StandardScaler preprocessing as logistic regression.

\paragraph{XGBoost.}
We use gradient-boosted trees (200 estimators, maximum depth 6). Clients train local ensembles and the server combines them by soft voting (averaging predicted probabilities); this is prediction-level ensembling, not parameter aggregation (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}).

All models use the same train/validation/test splits, and decision thresholds are optimized for recall on the validation sets of each model.

\subsection{Federated Aggregation Approaches}

\textbf{FedAvg}~\cite{mcmahan2017communication} averages client models weighted by local sample counts $n_k/n$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:erm}). Variants such as matched averaging~\cite{wang2020federated} may handle heterogeneity better, but FedAvg remains the standard baseline. It has two weaknesses relevant here: with non-uniform clients, larger clients dominate the global model, and a single malicious client can arbitrarily corrupt the average. \textbf{FedProx}~\cite{fedprox} adds a proximal term $\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^t\|^2$ to each client's local objective; it is a client-side modification, and the server still averages as in FedAvg. \textbf{Median} replaces the mean with a coordinate-wise median, which limits the influence of outlying updates. \textbf{Krum} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:krum}) scores each update by its distances to its nearest peers and returns the single update with the lowest score, i.e., the one that best matches the consensus of the other clients.

\subsection{Mathematical Formalism}

\subsubsection{Federated Learning Formulation}

We formulate FL as empirical risk minimization. With $K$ clients, where client $k$ holds data $\mathcal{D}_k$ of size $n_k$, the goal is to minimize
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} F(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k \cdot F_k(\mathbf{w}), \quad n = \sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k.
\label{eq:erm}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:fl_dp} summarizes the training pipeline: local training (with optional DP-SGD), followed by server-side aggregation with a pluggable rule. FedAvg, FedProx with FedAvg-style averaging, and Median and Krum applied to updates $\Delta\mathbf{w}$ are equivalent to applying the same rules to the client models themselves, because these rules commute with a common shift by $\mathbf{w}^t$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Federated training with optional DP-SGD and a pluggable aggregation rule. Raw data never leave the clients.}
\label{alg:fl_dp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Clients $k = 1, \ldots, K$; aggregation rule $\mathcal{A} \in \{$FedAvg, FedProx, FedF2, Median, Krum$\}$; optional DP parameters $(\sigma, C)$; rounds $T$
\State Initialize global model $\mathbf{w}^0$ at server
\For{round $t = 0$ to $T-1$}
  \For{each client $k$ in parallel}
    \State Initialize $\mathbf{w}_k \gets \mathbf{w}^t$ and train locally on $\mathcal{D}_k$ (for FedProx, add $\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^t\|^2$ to the local objective)
    \State \textbf{if} DP-SGD enabled \textbf{then} clip each per-sample gradient to norm $C$ and add Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2C^2\mathbf{I})$ to the summed gradient
    \State Send update $\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_k \gets \mathbf{w}_k - \mathbf{w}^t$ to server
  \EndFor
  \State Server receives updates from the responding set $R_t$
  \State $\Delta\mathbf{w}^t \gets \mathcal{A}(\{\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_k\}_{k \in R_t})$ \Comment{FedAvg: $\sum_{k\in R_t}\frac{n_k}{\sum_{j\in R_t}n_j}\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_k$}
  \State Update global model: $\mathbf{w}^{t+1} \gets \mathbf{w}^t + \Delta\mathbf{w}^t$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{Output:} Final model $\mathbf{w}^T$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Performance-Weighted Aggregation (FedF2)}

We study F2-score-based client weighting as an empirical baseline, not as a new algorithm. Weighting clients by local validation performance is a common heuristic; FedNova~\cite{wang2020fedavg}, by contrast, normalizes updates by the number of local steps to correct objective inconsistency. Our question is whether F2-based weighting improves on FedAvg for ICU mortality and where it fails under attack.

Let $F_{2,k}^{t}$ denote the F2-score of the local model $\mathbf{w}_{k}^{t}$ on client $k$'s own validation partition in round $t$. The aggregation weight is
\begin{equation}
\alpha_k^t = (1 - \gamma) \frac{n_k}{n} + \gamma \frac{F_{2,k}^t}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} F_{2,j}^t},
\label{eq:fedf2}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ controls the weight given to performance; $\gamma = 0$ recovers sample-size-weighted FedAvg, and $\sum_k \alpha_k^t = 1$. The global model is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k^t \mathbf{w}_k^t.
\end{equation}
The F2-score weights recall twice as heavily as precision:
\begin{equation}
F_2 = \frac{5 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{4 \cdot \text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}.
\end{equation}
Two properties limit what FedF2 can do. First, $\alpha_k^t \geq (1-\gamma)n_k/n$, so for $\gamma=0.5$ no client can fall below half of its FedAvg weight. Second, an always-positive client with clean validation labels has $F_2 = 5p/(4p+1) \approx 0.38$ at prevalence $p\approx 0.11$, only modestly below the roughly 0.47 of the pooled federated model (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation}); if its validation labels are poisoned as well, its F2 approaches 1 and the client is \emph{up}-weighted. Neither case offers meaningful protection (Section~\ref{sec:byzantine_robustness}).

\subsubsection{Coordinate-Wise Median Aggregation}

Let $\{\Delta \mathbf{w}^t_1, \ldots, \Delta \mathbf{w}^t_{K_{\text{respond}}}\}$ denote the updates received at round $t$. The coordinate-wise median is
\begin{equation}
[\text{Median}]_j = \text{median} \{ [\Delta \mathbf{w}^t_1]_j, \ldots, [\Delta \mathbf{w}^t_{K_{\text{respond}}}]_j \}
\end{equation}
for every coordinate $j = 1, \ldots, d$, where $d$ is the model dimension. A single malicious client can arbitrarily corrupt a mean, whereas the median stays within the range of the honest values as long as fewer than half of the clients are corrupted; Yin et al.~\cite{yin2018byzantine} give statistical error rates for median-type estimators under assumptions (e.g., bounded variance) that our heterogeneous clinical setting does not verify, so our evidence for Median is empirical.

\subsubsection{Krum Aggregation}

Krum~\cite{blanchard2017machine} selects the update closest to its peers. For each update $i$, let $\mathcal{N}_i$ be the $m$ updates nearest to it, with $m = K_{\text{respond}} - f - 2$ for an assumed bound $f$ on the number of Byzantine clients. The score is $s_i = \sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}\|\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_i-\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_j\|_2^2$ and the server returns $\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_{i^*}$ with $i^*=\arg\min_i s_i$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:krum}). The formal guarantee requires $2f+2 < K_{\text{respond}}$, i.e., $f \leq 2$ for $K=7$. Our implementation sets $m = K_{\text{respond}}-\lfloor\sqrt{K_{\text{respond}}}\rfloor-1 = 4$ for $K=7$, which corresponds to $f=1$ in this notation; results with two or three malicious clients are therefore empirical.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Krum selection}
\label{alg:krum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Updates $\{\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_k\}$, neighbor count $m$
\For{each client $i$}
  \State $s_i \gets \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} \|\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_i - \Delta\mathbf{w}^t_j\|_2^2$, where $\mathcal{N}_i$ are the $m$ nearest updates to $i$
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\Delta\mathbf{w}^t_{i^*}$, $i^* = \arg\min_i s_i$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Differential Privacy}
\label{sec:dp}

We apply DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016deep} at each client for the logistic-regression model, using the Gaussian mechanism~\cite{dwork2014algorithmic} inside the local optimizer rather than perturbing weights after training. For each local mini-batch (size 32), the client (1) computes per-sample gradients, (2) clips each to $\ell_2$ norm $C=1.0$, (3) sums the clipped gradients and adds Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2C^2\mathbf{I})$ with noise multiplier $\sigma\approx 4.80$, and (4) divides by the batch size before the gradient step. Clipping bounds each sample's influence independently of the number of parameters, which is why this design replaced our earlier output-perturbation prototype, whose noise scaled poorly with model dimensionality.

Privacy loss accumulates over all local steps and rounds and is tracked per client with a moments accountant~\cite{abadi2016deep}; with batch size 32, about 5,967 training samples per client (from unit cohorts averaging about 9,325 admissions), $C=1.0$, $\sigma\approx4.80$, and $\delta=10^{-5}$, the accumulated budget is $\epsilon=4.36$. This is example-level DP with respect to each client's released model updates. It does \emph{not} cover Platt scaling or threshold selection, which use validation labels, and it is applied only to the logistic-regression pipeline with FedAvg; the MLP and XGBoost results are non-private. Platt scaling is monotone and leaves AUROC unchanged.

\section{Results and Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure*}[t]

\caption{System scalability and network robustness in simulation. \textit{Left:} AUROC (blue, left axis) stays above 0.88 as the number of clients increases from 7 to 28, while throughput (orange, right axis) drops because of straggling under synchronous aggregation. \textit{Right:} AUROC under 10--30\% random client dropout; throughput rises with the dropout rate because fewer clients respond per round.}

We use two clinical databases. MIMIC-IV is used for model development, Byzantine-robustness testing, and internal validation; eICU-CRD is used for a federated replication on an independent multi-center database. In a clinical setting, overlooking a high-risk patient is costly: a false positive leads to additional monitoring or testing, whereas a false negative may mean that a deteriorating patient receives no escalation of care. We therefore report recall and precision at explicit operating points in addition to AUROC~\cite{saito2015precision}.

\subsection{Clinical Baseline vs.\ Federated Optimization}
\label{sec:baseline}

As a baseline, we trained class-weighted logistic regression on the pooled MIMIC-IV data (all 65,273 admissions, 31 variables). With the threshold chosen on the validation set ($\tau=0.39$), it reaches 85.2\% recall and 30.2\% precision; we treat this as the clinical reference operating point. Federated FedAvg with seven care-unit clients and no differential privacy reaches AUROC 0.8784. Its raw outputs are compressed toward zero (ECE 0.2338, Table~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation}), so the validation-optimal threshold is $\tau=0.05$ rather than 0.39; at $\tau=0.05$, recall is 86.3\% and precision 23.9\%.

\textbf{Matched-sensitivity comparison.} At about 85\% sensitivity, the centralized model has 30.2\% precision and the federated model 23.9\% (Table~\ref{tab:performance}). This precision gap, i.e., more false alarms per true detection, is a more informative cost of federation than the 1.52\% relative AUROC difference (0.8784 versus 0.8920).

\textbf{Calibrated, fixed threshold.} Applying Platt scaling to the federated outputs and keeping the centralized threshold $\tau=0.39$ gives AUROC 0.8784 (unchanged, since Platt scaling is monotone), recall 43.5\%, and precision 69.8\%. Calibration fixes the probabilities (ECE 0.0091) but $\tau=0.39$ is then a different operating point; sensitivity depends on threshold selection, and a threshold transferred from a centralized model should not be assumed to preserve sensitivity. Calibrated metrics are used for the comparisons in this paper unless noted. In our runs, validation performance plateaued after about five rounds (curves not shown). In the simulation, only model updates are exchanged between clients and the server.

\textbf{Statistical note.} Only FedAvg was repeated over five seeds (standard deviation 0.0065 AUROC); the centralized-versus-federated gap (0.0136) is about two such standard deviations, and the centralized model was not repeated over the same seeds. All other results are single runs. We did not run DeLong~\cite{delong1988comparing} or bootstrap tests, so AUROC differences smaller than about 0.01 (such as FedAvg 0.8784 versus FedF2 0.8781) should be treated as indistinguishable, and Tables~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation} and~\ref{tab:byzantine_robustness} as indicative.

\textbf{FedProx.} With $\mu=0.01$, FedProx obtained AUROC 0.8591, recall 38.0\%, and precision 63.0\% under the same Platt-scaled protocol. FedProx's AUROC is below FedAvg's in every configuration we ran (0.8591 in Table~\ref{tab:performance}, 0.8174 in Table~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation}, and 0.7721 in Table~\ref{tab:byzantine_robustness}), and the two nominally comparable 5-round Platt-scaled runs differ between Tables~\ref{tab:performance} and~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation}. A small proximal weight should make FedProx behave close to FedAvg, so this gap and its instability suggest sensitivity to implementation or hyperparameters that we did not isolate. We therefore treat the FedProx results as preliminary and draw no conclusion about its intrinsic merits.

\subsection{Performance-Weighted Aggregation (FedF2)}
\label{sec:fedf2_results}

We compare FedF2 with FedAvg and FedProx under clean and poisoned conditions. This is an empirical analysis of an existing weighting heuristic, not a new algorithm. We use two additional metrics. \textbf{AUPRC} summarizes the precision--recall curve and ignores true negatives. \textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)} measures the gap between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies over $B$ bins:
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|B_b|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_b) - \text{conf}(B_b) \right|.
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:clinical_aggregation} presents the ablation under clean conditions and with one degenerate client. The degenerate client is simulated by corrupting the labels of the second-largest unit, Cardiac Vascular ICU ($n=8{,}118$), so that 99.9\% are positive; this mimics a node that fails to train and labels every patient as deceased, which gives perfect recall and no precision.

This follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:fedf2}: the degenerate client's weight cannot fall below $0.5\,n_k/n \approx 6.2\%$, and with the F2 values above it stays close to its FedAvg share of $n_k/n\approx 12.4\%$; if the client's validation labels are corrupted as well, its F2 rises and its weight increases. We therefore regard FedF2 as a negative result: no measurable benefit in clean conditions and no protection against poisoning. \subsubsection{XGBoost: Gradient Boosting in a Federated Setting}
XGBoost has the highest centralized AUROC (0.9243). With ensemble soft voting, the federated version reaches 0.9155, a relative loss of 0.95\% and 0.045 AUROC above the federated MLP (0.8701). Because voting averages predicted probabilities, no alignment of model parameters across Non-IID clients is needed, unlike weight averaging. The result holds with an average of about 5,967 training records per client, which is enough for each client to train a useful local model; we did not test smaller clients.

\textbf{Privacy notice.} Soft voting shares locally trained tree models or their predictions rather than gradients, so the privacy properties differ from gradient-based FL~\cite{zhu2019deep}. The DP-SGD guarantee in Table~\ref{tab:performance} applies only to the logistic-regression pipeline and does not extend to XGBoost; bounding the privacy loss of prediction aggregation requires additional analysis beyond the scope of this paper~\cite{shokri2016membership}. All models are evaluated on held-out MIMIC-IV test data. A real deployment would additionally require encryption or secure aggregation.

\subsubsection{Comparison: LR, MLP, XGBoost}

Table~\ref{tab:multimodel_comparison} shows the centralized AUROC ranking XGBoost (0.9243) $>$ MLP (0.9185) $>$ LR (0.8914), but the federated losses differ: XGBoost 0.95\% (0.9243 $\rightarrow$ 0.9155), logistic regression 1.48\% (0.8914 $\rightarrow$ 0.8782), and MLP 5.27\% (0.9185 $\rightarrow$ 0.8701). A plausible explanation for the MLP's larger loss is that averaging weights of a non-convex model trained on heterogeneous clients mixes inconsistent representations; we did not test this. All three federated models reach recall above 80\% at their validation-optimized thresholds. For deployment, models with a small federated loss, such as XGBoost with soft voting, are attractive, subject to the privacy caveat above.

\subsection{Byzantine Robustness under Diverse Attack Scenarios}
\label{sec:byzantine_robustness}

Byzantine-robust aggregation rules have sound theoretical motivation~\cite{blanchard2017machine, yin2018byzantine}, but their behavior in multi-client healthcare FL has been studied less. We compare all five aggregation rules under three kinds of attack on 1, 2, or 3 of the 7 clients.

\subsubsection{Attack Scenarios and Baselines}

We evaluate five aggregation rules across eight scenarios: (i) a \textit{clean baseline}; (ii) \textit{label-flip attacks} (1, 2, or 3 poisoned clients) in which compromised clients invert all local labels; (iii) \textit{sign-flip attacks} (1 or 2 clients) with oversampling of the positive class; and (iv) \textit{adaptive attacks} (1 or 2 clients) that combine sign flipping, label flipping, and positive-class oversampling. The rules are: \textit{FedAvg} (sample-size-weighted), \textit{FedProx} ($\mu=0.01$), \textit{Median}, \textit{Krum}, and \textit{FedF2} ($\gamma=0.5$). To study convergence and resilience, we train for 20 rounds (versus 5 in Table~\ref{tab:performance}) without Platt scaling. The attacks are not tailored to Median or Krum, so they do not constitute a worst-case adversary.

\textbf{Median and Krum are the most robust} (Table~\ref{tab:byzantine_robustness}, Fig.~\ref{fig:byzantine_robustness}). Under three label-flip clients, Median keeps AUROC at 0.8594 (0.4\% loss) and Krum at 0.8511 (1.6\% loss); across all tested attacks, Median's minimum is 0.8353 (adaptive, two clients) and Krum's is 0.8511. Median sometimes exceeds its own clean value (0.8698 under one label-flip client and 0.8710 under one adaptive client versus 0.8628); such differences of about 0.01 are comparable to the seed variability we measured for FedAvg and should not be over-interpreted.

Krum returns a single client's update, so its AUROC equals that of one client-trained model. It yields 0.8653 in the clean run and in every one-attacker run, i.e., it selects the same update with and without attackers, and 0.8511 in every two- and three-attacker run. This explains the identical values across attack types. It also means that Krum's score must be read against a local-only baseline, which we did not run: in a single-institution simulation a single client's model can already be competitive with the federated models. Krum's formal guarantee requires $2f+2<K$, i.e., $f\le 2$ for $K=7$; the three-client results are empirical, and our neighbor count corresponds to $f=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{FedAvg} collapses to AUROC 0.5011 under three label-flip clients (43.0\% loss) because label-flipped updates corrupt the average and averaging offers no correction.

\textbf{FedProx} is the most affected: AUROC is 0.2953 (61.8\% loss) under three label-flip clients. A value below 0.5 indicates a systematically inverted ranking, consistent with models that learned the flipped labels, rather than random behavior. Given the instability of FedProx across our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), these numbers are specific to $\mu=0.01$ and 20 uncalibrated rounds, and we report no $\mu$ sweep.
% TODO(authors): if the mu in {0.001, 0.01, 0.1} sweep was actually run, add its results as a table; otherwise leave as is.

\textbf{FedF2} is comparable to FedAvg without attacks and with one attacker (AUROC about 0.855), but falls to 0.4000 under three label-flip clients (54.4\% loss). Poisoned clients also corrupt their local validation sets, so F2-based weights cannot identify them (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Sign-flip attacks are weaker.} No method falls below AUROC 0.696. For FedAvg, the mean loss is 0.7\% over the sign-flip scenarios versus 18.3\% over the label-flip scenarios; for FedProx, 6.8\% versus 51.6\%. Under three label-flip clients, the ordering is Median $\approx$ Krum $\gg$ FedAvg $\gg$ FedF2 $\gg$ FedProx. FedF2 therefore cannot rival the robust rules, and for any environment with credible adversarial risk we recommend Median aggregation.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Federated Replication on eICU-CRD}
\label{subsec:eicu_validation}

To test whether the federated behavior observed on MIMIC-IV recurs on an independent multi-center database, we ran a replication on eICU-CRD~\cite{eicu2018}, a public database of over 200,000 ICU admissions from 208 hospitals. We selected 131,517 adult admissions ($\geq18$ years, length of stay $\geq4$ hours) and the seven hospitals with the highest admission volume (hospitals 73, 264, 338, 420, 243, 458, 167), yielding 22,361 training samples across 7 clients. The mortality rate was 10.1\% (MIMIC-IV: 10.8\%), the mean age was $61.4 \pm 16.5$ years (MIMIC-IV: $64.5\pm15.8$), and 29 clinical features from the first 24 hours were aligned using the same preprocessing pipeline. New models were trained on eICU-CRD; the MIMIC-IV model was not applied to eICU-CRD.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centralized baseline:} AUROC 0.8441 (MIMIC-IV: 0.8920).
    \item \textbf{Federated FedAvg:} AUROC 0.8349, a 1.09\% relative loss versus centralized.
    \item \textbf{Calibration:} ECE 0.0134 after Platt scaling.
    \item \textbf{Inter-dataset gap:} the centralized AUROC differs by 0.048 (5.4\% relative) between MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD.
\end{itemize}

The 1.09\% federated loss on eICU-CRD is similar to the 1.52\% on MIMIC-IV, so the small AUROC cost of federation recurred in a second database. The 0.048 gap between datasets cannot be attributed to demographic or practice differences alone, because the datasets also differ in feature count (29 versus 31), sample size, and preprocessing.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Simulation scope.} The seven MIMIC-IV clients are care units of one hospital. The eICU-CRD experiment trains new models on a second database; transferring the MIMIC-IV model to eICU-CRD hospitals, i.e., true external validation, was not evaluated.

\textbf{Statistics.} Most results are single runs; only FedAvg was repeated over five seeds, and the variability found there (0.0065 AUROC) is comparable to many differences between methods. We report no confidence intervals or DeLong tests and no local-only baseline, which is needed to interpret Krum, whose output is one client's model.

\textbf{Privacy.} DP-SGD was evaluated only for logistic regression with FedAvg at a single $\epsilon$. Platt scaling and threshold selection use validation labels outside the guarantee, and DP can affect minority classes disproportionately~\cite{bagdasaryan2019differential}. XGBoost ensembling has no formal privacy guarantee. DP and Byzantine-robust aggregation were not evaluated together, and DP noise may interact with Krum's distances and the median; this remains open.

\textbf{Threat model.} The attacks are simple and not tailored to the aggregator. Krum's formal guarantee covers $f\le2$ for seven clients, and FedProx results were unstable across experiments.

\textbf{Deployment.} The simulation assumes synchronous rounds, harmonized features, and no network layer. Real deployments also need secure transport and aggregation, audit trails, ontology harmonization (e.g., ICD-9/10), and prospective clinical validation, including subgroup and fairness analysis. The study is retrospective and does not assess clinical utility.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We benchmarked federated learning for ICU mortality prediction on MIMIC-IV, with a federated replication on eICU-CRD. Federated logistic regression reached 0.8784 AUROC versus 0.8920 centralized (1.52\% relative loss), and the replication showed a similar small loss (1.09\%; 0.8349 versus 0.8441). The AUROC difference understates the practical cost: at matched sensitivity of about 85\%, federated precision was 23.9\% versus 30.2\%, and a Platt-scaled federated model at the centralized threshold $\tau=0.39$ had only 43.5\% recall, so thresholds must be re-selected for federated models. DP-SGD ($\epsilon=4.36$) gave 0.8477 AUROC in a single run.

Under label-flip, sign-flip, and adaptive attacks on 1--3 of 7 clients, Median kept AUROC at or above 0.8353 (0.8594 under three label-flip clients) and Krum at about 0.851, while FedAvg and FedProx fell to 0.5011 and 0.2953 and FedF2 to 0.4000. F2-based weighting showed no benefit over FedAvg. These results support Byzantine-robust aggregation when client trust cannot be assured. They come from single-split simulations without joint DP-plus-robust-aggregation experiments, statistical tests, or external validation, and they do not establish clinical readiness.
