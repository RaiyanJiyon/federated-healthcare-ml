\begin{abstract}
One common bottleneck that has frustrated predictive AI efforts in healthcare is the isolated nature of patient data. Because of stringent regulations, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the U.S. and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU, clinics are generally forbidden from simply sending copies of their data over to a server in one location, which prevents training a single predictive model based on combined patient information. While Federated Learning (FL) proposes a solid workaround - namely, by bringing the model to the data - it's seldom studied under the conditions encountered in practice: i.e., real hospitals.

For this paper, we present a Federated Learning framework tailored for clinical use cases, by leveraging the benchmark Pima Indians Diabetes Database (768 records, 8 features of clinical information, with 34.9\% positive records) with its relevance in real scenarios. Firstly, we replicate the typical differences between demographic representations present in different hospitals and simulate data distribution over the proxies network by generating Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) data partitioning using Dirichlet distribution ($ \alpha = 0.5 $). Following that, we protect the network using Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) and improve system robustness against malicious data poisoning by utilizing a secure aggregation strategy such as Byzantine-robust aggregation (specifically, Krum, Median).

Our approach distinguished itself through an algorithmic pivot. Whereas earlier methods emphasized overall accuracy, we prioritized clinical safety above all else. By tweaking several user-defined classification thresholds and local interactions, our federated system, even with stringent Non-IID distribution of client data, reaches a recall rate of 73.70\% \( \pm \)16.95\%. This value is surprisingly close to the 87.04\% Recall of the centralized, and thus non-federated, approach. Finally, to address concerns about privacy-utility compromise, we provide extensive empirical verification: the approach with DP-SGD on our Non-IID restricted dataset introduces a genuine drop in model performance. However, we show that appropriate robust aggregation mechanisms (e.g., Krum, Median) improve the robustness of the federated learning process against such Byzantine threats. This work establishes an honest, reproducible benchmark for evaluating federated healthcare systems under realistic constraints.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The digitalization of healthcare systems has generated massive repositories of high-dimensional clinical data, presenting an unprecedented opportunity for diagnostic machine learning integrations \cite{nguyen2022federated}. However, the development of functional, cross-demographic predictive models has historically required compiling sweeping datasets into centralized servers---a paradigm that has recently suffered significant limitations. With ransomware attacks and massive hospital data breaches skyrocketing, storing sensitive diagnostics in centralized cloud environments has become a major security concern \cite{mothukuri2021survey}. Consequently, this architecture is now challenging to reconcile with modern data protection regulations including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Patient records are consequently trapped in isolated data silos across varying, independent proxy facilities, effectively starving centralized AI models of foundational training data and posing severe risks to responsible clinical machine learning \cite{price2019privacy, wiens2019do}.

To avoid these risks, Federated Learning (FL) has been proposed as a solution \cite{mcmahan2017communication}, wherein multiple medical proxy systems can train model weights on their own static, raw data locally and only send model parameter updates to a central server to aggregate the parameters. In FL for healthcare, sensitive patient data isn't transferred from institutions to any external server; instead, only model training updates are communicated, ensuring that patient records never leave their host institution \cite{kairouz2021advances}. Comprehensive reviews and surveys have been published on FL in healthcare that describe and analyze FL architectures and evaluate potential applications in clinical settings, such as the use of FL with electronic health records (EHR) \cite{xu2021federated}, smart health monitoring (SHM) \cite{nguyen2022federated}, and multi-institutional clinical collaboration \cite{antunes2022federated, sheller2020federated}.

Unfortunately, applying FL to real healthcare networks faces significant statistical challenges. Baseline algorithms like Federated Averaging (FedAvg) are primarily designed and evaluated against standard Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) public data \cite{li2020federated_challenges}. When federating over hospitals operating in different regions, these datasets present extreme variations in terms of demographics and class imbalance, consequently falling into the Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) setting \cite{zhao2018federated}. Standard averaging algorithms become unstable, suffer from client drift, and quickly result in poor model performance; hence, tailored heterogeneous optimization is required \cite{fedprox, scaffold}. This challenge is magnified in healthcare datasets, where disease prevalence can vary by more than 100x between hospitals \cite{li2020federated}.

Moreover, while decentralization helps to reduce bulk data breaches, fundamental transmission paradigms remain susceptible to gradient inversion attacks, in which adversaries can reconstruct sensitive patient data from shared model updates \cite{zhu2019deep}. These attacks compromise the privacy promise of FL, which lacks explicitly defined mathematical boundaries \cite{dwork2014algorithmic, abadi2016deep}. In this study, we design and test a fully secured, privacy-focused Federated Learning pipeline using the Pima Indians Diabetes Database \cite{smith1988using}. We address strict healthcare constraints to demonstrate empirically the viability of secure, distributed health analytics within adversarial networks. The significant contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) distribution based on the Dirichlet distribution to simulate the inherently discrete hospital data silos.
    \item We natively use Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) and Byzantine-robust aggregation strategies such as Krum and Median to protect against both gradient information leakage and poisoning attacks.
    \item We prioritize the recall of critical diagnostic information over central gross accuracy. We demonstrate that comparable safety can be maintained in a federated manner, coupled with an analysis of the privacy-utility relationship and corresponding trade-offs across differing privacy budgets (\( \epsilon \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \)).
\end{itemize}

The remaining paper is organized as follows: Section 2 briefly reviews the relevant prior work and indicates the existing theoretical gaps. Section 3 elaborates on our approach: the data distribution, robust aggregation technique and the privacy constraints on the training dataset. Section 4 presents the results from our experiments and benchmarking of its scalability. Lastly, Section 5 offers concluding remarks and outlines future research directions.

\section{Related Work}

Recent interest in Federated Learning as a component of healthcare analytics has been strong because of its inherent capability to support data sovereignty and multi-institutional collaboration. Initial systems deployments showed demonstrable effectiveness in complex diagnostics systems, such as federated medical imaging, privacy-preserving brain tumour segmentation \cite{rieke2020future, sheller2020brain}, and explainable federated diagnostics for pneumonia detection \cite{biswas2025flpnexainet}. Extensive systemic surveys by Nguyen et al. \cite{nguyen2022federated} and Antunes et al. \cite{antunes2022federated} show that, although FL radically advances institutional access to data without interfering with patient privacy, the translation of standard mathematical frameworks to dedicated clinical settings is fundamentally limited by the chaotic nature of medical data. Moreover, Xu et al. \cite{xu2021federated} showed that federated models of electronic health records can achieve predictive accuracy and retain a tight control over data, but their experiments also showed enduring difficulties when clinical features have inter-institutional distributional drift.

There is no central challenge in federation, as federation is generally preferred; it may be difficult, but it's worth it. A core challenge in federated learning over healthcare data is that typical clinical data possesses high diagnostic imbalance and heterogeneity in patient demographics. When standard FedAvg runs under Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) assumptions, standard FedAvg severely degrades, diverges drastically, and can't be trained even after one round \cite{fedavg_convergence}. Zhao et al. \cite{zhao2018federated} confirmed that accuracy drops by as much as 55\% in an extreme Non-IID scenario compared to standard FedAvg with data sampled from the same hospital. To address this issue, some literature has tried adding proximal terms to the standard optimization to keep weights local \cite{fedprox} or adding terms like control variates \cite{scaffold}. Such a performance degradation has been conclusively quantified in experimental benchmarking studies across highly skewed data silos, and is the reason why sophisticated architectural boundaries are deployed which directly map to realistic data fragmentation \cite{li2020federated, li2020federated_challenges}.

At the same time, these decentralized models need to consider malicious network effects which may contain targeted data-poisoning attacks or compromised proxy nodes. Mothukuri et al. \cite{mothukuri2021survey} present a detailed taxonomy of security threats unique to FL, and classify attacks as either data poisoning (corrupting local training data) or model poisoning (manipulating transmitted model updates). Specialized gradient inversion methods, such as those demonstrated by Zhu et al. \cite{zhu2019deep}, have been shown to be able to reconstruct individual training samples using shared gradients, and this places extreme risk of patient confidentiality, even in ostensibly privacy-preserving architectures. Therefore, state-of-the-art literature has shifted to Byzantine-robust aggregation policies to actively sieve aberrant client uploads. Although more complex meta-aggregators such as Bulyan \cite{mhamdi2018bulyan} provide compounding defense by recursive evaluation, they add significantly to computational overhead. Thus, under-funded hospital edge server operational policies that are highly optimized single-pass filtering policies such as Krum \cite{blanchard2017machine} and coordinate-wise Median \cite{yin2018byzantine} are the best operational balance under under-funding and the strict operational limits of Differential Privacy \cite{abadi2016deep}.

Although these unequal explicit realizations reveal a high level of theoretical effectiveness in countering certain threat types in the network (i.e., the handling of Non-IID data or the presence of Byzantine attackers as a matter-of-course), an integrated synthesis that is mapped to real clinical results has not been defined \cite{nguyen2022federated}. Particularly, the way in which these joint optimization constraints affect patient-focused algorithmic Recall during binary medical classification is a critical gap in existing research literature. In the analysis of highly imbalanced, life-critical data, precision-recall optimization offers much more informative measures of evaluation than standard accuracy or ROC measures \cite{saito2015precision}. Within the present research, we give a combined empirical analysis involving Non-IID optimization, Differential Privacy, and Byzantine robustness, and in the process, fundamentally tune the architecture to optimize Clinical Recall, a safety blind spot that this paper explicitly addresses.

\section{Methodology}
To thoroughly evaluate the performance of Federated Learning (FL) under realistic clinical constraints, we architected a distributed framework simulating independent healthcare proxies. This methodology section details the system orchestration, our implementation of class-imbalanced data distribution, and the deployment of advanced defense strategies.

\subsection{System Architecture and Reproducibility}
The system utilized a custom client-server simulation framework, with each client leveraging Scikit-Learn instances for local computation. To ensure the reproducibility and consistency of our findings, experiments were strictly controlled using a pseudo-random seed value of 42 and a standardized 80\% train / 20\% test stratified data split. Furthermore, to strictly eliminate single-run variance and confirm statistical validation, all reported performance metrics represent the arithmetic mean (\( \pm \) standard deviation) evaluated across 5 independent Non-IID data partition trials using seeds \{42, 123, 456, 789, 2024\}. System-level simulations were executed on standard CPU-centric virtual nodes to accurately emulate the restricted computing resources commonly observed in real edge hospital environments. For the baseline architecture, the global environment synchronizes across 5 decentralized proxy clients over 10 communication rounds. To handle the highly imbalanced nature of our dataset, each proxy trains a localized Logistic Regression model optimized using the limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm. Models are trained iteratively with a learning rate of \( \eta = 0.01 \) and a maximum convergence threshold of 2000 iterations to guarantee local stability before transmission. Importantly, raw patient data are never ever transferred but only updated model coefficients are serially and safely aggregated by the central coordinator.

\subsection{Distributed Patient Cohorts (Non-IID Modeling)}
The key weakness of standard ML research is that they assume that datasets are Independent and Identically Distributed (IID). To effectively test our framework, we tested the Pima Indians Diabetes Database which includes 768 samples of patients mapping 8 raw clinical features. The cohort exhibits a profound natural class imbalance, with positive diabetes cases comprising exactly 34.9\% (268) of the dataset. Because specialized hospitals treat highly varying patient demographics, we deliberately fragmented these 768 samples by explicitly implementing a non-IID distribution split across horizontal clients.

We utilized a Dirichlet distribution to partition the target vectors across the decentralized clients. By applying a concentration parameter of \( \alpha = 0.5 \), the simulated facilities receive vastly different densities of diabetic versus non-diabetic patients. Consequently, local models are subjected to unequal representation biases, severely testing the robustness of the global aggregation schema and proving its capability to learn generalizing traits from highly heterogeneous medical data silos.

\subsection{Federated Aggregation Strategies}
In establishing the federated baseline, the central server aggregates local model updates utilizing the standard Federated Averaging (\textit{FedAvg}) protocol. However, aggregating weights from non-IID demographics directly exposes the global model to optimization drift. 

To counteract this and provide resilience against corrupted proxies, we implemented advanced Byzantine-robust defense policies. Specifically, we evaluate the performance offsets when transitioning from basic averaging to \textit{Median} computation (which natively discards extreme distributional outliers) and \textit{Krum} aggregation. Krum successfully fortifies the global model by returning the specific client weight vector that fundamentally minimizes the distance to a subset of its neighboring vectors, thus mathematically neutralizing artificially poisoned clients. We intentionally constrain our empirical baseline to FedAvg supplemented with Byzantine-resilient operators, explicitly reserving the implementation of computationally heavy proximal limiters (such as FedProx or SCAFFOLD) for future scaling research. This laboratory separation enables us to carefully measure the new mathematical interactions between Differential Privacy and geometric gradient-distance defenses.

\subsection{Differential Privacy Constraints}
We used Differential Privacy Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD) to meet the high standards of compliance with healthcare privacy regulations. Deterministically added Gaussian noise is injected into the gradients during local model updates, before serializing transmissions to the global server---an approach structurally validated across modern privacy-preserving medical classification literature \cite{abadi2016deep, hasan2025privacy}. Using a strict privacy budget configuration of \( \epsilon = 1.0 \) with a failure probability of \( \delta = 0.01 \), and a purposeful gradient clipping norm threshold of 1.0, the framework provides a formal mathematical guarantee that adversarial reconstruction of specific client patient records by gradient leakage is computationally unfeasible. To fully describe the privacy-utility trade-off, we measured system performance over a variety of privacy budgets (\( \epsilon \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\} \)). Our empirical results indicate that DP-SGD incurs a significant performance cost on this constrained set of data: at \( \epsilon = 1.0 \), accuracy has been reduced to 62.34\% (\( \pm \)6.43\%), and Recall has been reduced to 52.22\% (\( \pm \)15.86\%). The high noise-to-signal ratio (\( \sigma \approx 3.03 \)) relative to the small model parameter space (19 features) mathematically contributes to the degradation in applying formal DP guarantees to small-scale tabular datasets. Table~\ref{tab:performance} gives a detailed privacy-utility curve to transparently measure this trade-off.

Since the application of diagnostics is in a deployment scenario, our evaluation model conclusively contrasts with traditional ML evaluations, where gross accuracy is not prioritized. In clinical settings, a false negative (missed diagnosis) is much more disastrous than a false positive (which merely results in additional testing).

\subsection{Clinical Baseline vs. Federated Optimization}
The centralized and federated configurations were compared using the same preprocessing, feature engineering (11 engineered features: 5 interaction terms, 4 polynomial features, and 2 ratio features, e.g., Glucose \( \times \) BMI), and a clinical decision threshold of 0.30. The centralized baseline had an accuracy of 69.48\% and a Recall of 87.04\% (7 missed diabetic patients in 54 cases), defining a robust upper limit of clinical safety on this dataset.

With federated conditions and Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) Dirichlet partitioning (\( \alpha = 0.5 \)), the FL baseline (FedAvg, no DP) had a similar accuracy of 70.91\% (\( \pm \)1.90\%) but a larger variance in Recall of 73.70\% (\( \pm \)16.95\%) with an average of 14.2 (\( \pm \)9.2) missed patients across 5 independent partition trials. This difference can be directly explained by the fact that the Dirichlet partitioning process is stochastic: some Non-IID splits can result in client distributions in which the samples of the minority classes are concentrated among a small number of clients, restricting the global model's ability to learn strong positive-class decision boundaries. Performance trajectory monitoring confirmed that global convergence strictly stabilized at 10 communication rounds. As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:performance}, while the federated model does not exceed centralized Recall, it achieves operationally comparable diagnostic performance while preserving complete data locality---a critical requirement for multi-institutional healthcare deployments.

\subsection{Privacy and Scalability Efficiency Trade-Offs}
While DP-SGD ensures gradient security and Non-IID Dirichlet distribution guarantees demographic realism, they induce a significant and honest performance taxation. As demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:performance}, increasing the privacy budget from \( \epsilon = 10.0 \) (moderate privacy) to \( \epsilon = 1.0 \) (strong privacy) reduces mean Recall from 78.89\% to 52.22\%. This substantial degradation is a fundamental consequence of the high noise-to-signal ratio inherent in applying formal Gaussian DP mechanisms to small-parameter models (19 features), and represents an important empirical finding for practitioners considering DP deployment on constrained tabular healthcare data. Our scalability testing evaluated system convergence across configurations scaling from 5 incrementally up to 20 clients concurrently (see Fig.~\ref{fig:comprehensive}). The system demonstrates asymptotic convergence efficiency, wherein communication latency scales linearly with $N$ clients (see Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). However, Recall varied significantly across client counts---only the 15-client configuration consistently maintained clinical safety (\( \geq \)80\% Recall), while 5, 7, 10, and 20-client configurations fell below this threshold. This instability is mathematically attributed to the interaction between Non-IID partitioning stochasticity and dataset size constraints; with only 768 total samples, certain partitions produce critically imbalanced local datasets. To further validate realistic operational constraints, we systematically simulated unannounced client dropouts mid-round; the global architecture maintained zero structural degradation, requiring only standard fallback omission to complete the aggregation round (see Fig.~\ref{fig:bottleneck}). We observed that implementing Byzantine robust aggregations such as Krum requires roughly a 15-20\% increase in required communication rounds to reach standard stabilization, presenting a quantifiable trade-off between absolute system safety and bandwidth optimization.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper thoroughly describes a reproducible evaluation that addresses several key weaknesses and potential risks present in the previous state-of-the-art, offering robust evidence of its utility while clearly outlining its boundaries. To more accurately depict a clinical setting, the evaluation framework uses skewed Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) data generated to resemble differences between hospitals (which are much higher in clinical practice) and to prevent the authors from engaging in misleading studies on fully independent identical data. 

Our proposed FL solution yields accuracy comparable to a well-trained centralized model (70.91\% accuracy vs. 74.11\% accuracy of training on the entire dataset). Most importantly, it satisfies the need for absolute data locality demanded by current healthcare regulations. The primary takeaways from our work are: 1) high accuracy alone doesn't tell the full story for medical ML models; by adjusting decision thresholds appropriately and conducting localized feature engineering, both federated and centralized models achieved sufficient Recall for clinical applications. 2) Unfortunately, our results present fair and honest evidence of DP-SGD with formal \( \epsilon = 1.0 \) guarantees, resulting in a significant drop in Recall in small-scale tabular data from 73.70\% to 52.22\%, which may indicate the current cost of deploying DP guarantees at scale in healthcare. Table~\ref{tab:performance} provides an example of where to start for privacy budgets appropriate for specific needs based on institutional risk. While Pima was a useful dataset to test basic methods, there wasn't enough data for reliable validation as it is small-scale, historic, and tabular. Much larger multi-modal clinical datasets should be used to test DP in the real world. In such large-scale datasets, the noise produced by DP methods is expected to be much smaller, meaning higher privacy without compromising clinical efficacy.
